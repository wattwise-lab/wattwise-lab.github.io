Cumulative saving over time

AfterSaved vs staying put

Cumulative saving counts only months where both loans are being repaid. After either loan ends, the comparison changes. Closing costs are recovered once cumulative saving passes them — that month is the break-even point.

Formula

Monthly payment = balance × monthly rate × (1 + monthly rate)payments ÷ [(1 + monthly rate)payments − 1]. Break-even months = closing costs ÷ monthly saving. Total interest = monthly payment × payments − balance.

Worked example

Balance $300,000, current 7% with 20 years left: payment $2,325.90. New 5% for 20 years: payment $1,979.87, saving $346.03/month. With $4,000 closing costs, break-even is $4,000 ÷ $346.03 = 11.56 months. Total interest falls from $258,215.23 to $175,168.13 — a $83,047.10 saving — and break-even arrives well inside a 10-year stay, so on these numbers the refinance pays off.

Scope and limitations

Both loans are fixed-rate with no fees beyond the closing-cost input, no cash-out, and no change in balance at refinancing. Taxes, insurance, HOA dues, and private mortgage insurance are not included. A longer new term can lower the payment while raising total interest — the comparison table shows that trade directly. Rate changes, points bought separately from closing costs, and tax effects are not modeled.

FAQ

Is a lower payment always a win?

No. Extending the term can cut the monthly payment but add years of interest. Compare total interest as well as the monthly figure — the table above does both.

What does break-even mean?

The month when cumulative monthly savings first cover closing costs. If you move or refinance again before break-even, the refinance may not have paid off.

What if I move before break-even?

Then the savings never repay the closing costs. Enter your planned stay above and the verdict checks this for you: a refinance that breaks even after your move is flagged, even if the monthly saving looks good.

Does refinancing reset my payoff date?

Yes, unless the new term matches your remaining term. A new 20-year loan on a mortgage with 20 years left keeps the date; a new 30-year loan pushes it out 10 years, which is usually why total interest rises despite a lower payment.

Should I include points?

Yes, in closing costs. Points buy a lower rate, so they belong in the break-even calculation, not as a separate saving.

Does this cover adjustable rates?

No. It assumes the new rate stays fixed for the whole new term. If the new rate can adjust, the saving and break-even figures are only valid until the first adjustment.
