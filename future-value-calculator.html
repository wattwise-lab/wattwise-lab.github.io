Formula

Future value = present value × (1 + annual rate ÷ compounding periods)compounding periods × years. Interest earned = future value − present value. Growth multiple = future value ÷ present value. The default compounding frequency is 1 (annual); use 12 for monthly compounding.

Worked example

$10,000 at 5% compounded annually for 10 years: future value = $10,000 × 1.0510 = $16,288.95. Interest earned is $16,288.95 − $10,000 = $6,288.95, and the growth multiple is 1.63×. After 5 years the same sum is worth $12,762.82.

Scope and limitations

This is a single lump sum at a fixed rate — no regular deposits or withdrawals. For regular payments, use a savings-goal or annuity calculation instead. The rate is assumed constant for the whole period; real investment returns vary year to year. Taxes, fees, and inflation are not included, so the result is a nominal future amount, not purchasing power.

FAQ

Does compounding frequency matter much?

Yes, but less than rate and time. Monthly compounding on the example gives $16,470.09 instead of $16,288.95 — about $181 more over 10 years. Daily compounding adds only a little more again.

What is the difference from compound interest?

None in the math: compound interest is the interest earned, future value is the total. This page shows both so you do not have to subtract by hand.

Can the rate be zero?

Yes. At 0% the future value equals the present value and no interest is earned.

Does this account for inflation?

No. To compare purchasing power, divide the future value by (1 + inflation rate)years, or use an inflation-adjusted calculation.

What about regular monthly deposits?

They are not included here. A lump sum plus deposits needs the future value of an annuity added on top — a separate calculation with its own formula.

Why do small rate differences matter so much?

Because growth compounds: each year's interest earns interest in later years. Over 30 years, 6% instead of 5% on $10,000 gives $57,434.91 instead of $43,219.42.
