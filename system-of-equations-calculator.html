Formula

For a₁x + b₁y = c₁ and a₂x + b₂y = c₂, let D = a₁b₂ − a₂b₁. If D ≠ 0, there is exactly one solution: x = (c₁b₂ − c₂b₁) ÷ D and y = (a₁c₂ − a₂c₁) ÷ D. This is Cramer's rule for two equations. If D = 0, the lines are parallel or identical: parallel lines never meet (no solution), identical lines meet everywhere (infinitely many solutions).

Worked example

2x + y = 7 and x − y = 2: D = 2 × (−1) − 1 × 1 = −3. Then x = (7 × (−1) − 2 × 1) ÷ (−3) = (−9) ÷ (−3) = 3, and y = (2 × 2 − 1 × 7) ÷ (−3) = (−3) ÷ (−3) = 1. Check: 2 × 3 + 1 = 7, and 3 − 1 = 2. Both equations hold, so (3, 1) is the single crossing point of the two lines.

Scope and limitations

This page solves two linear equations in two unknowns. It does not handle three or more equations, nonlinear terms (x², xy), or inequalities. When D = 0 it distinguishes the two cases: if the equations are proportional (same line), it reports infinitely many solutions; otherwise it reports no solution. Results display to two decimals; repeating decimals such as 1/3 show as 0.33, so keep the fraction form for exact work.

FAQ

What does the solution mean geometrically?

Each equation is a straight line, and the solution is their crossing point. One crossing means one solution; parallel lines never cross (no solution); the same line twice means every point on it works (infinitely many solutions).

What if the determinant is zero?

Then the two lines have the same slope. The page checks proportionality: if one equation is a multiple of the other, the lines coincide and every point on the line is a solution; otherwise they are distinct parallels with no solution at all.

Why is it called Cramer's rule?

After Gabriel Cramer, who published the determinant method in 1750. It is the same elimination you would do by hand — multiply, subtract, solve — compressed into two formulas.

Can I solve by substitution instead?

Yes, and you should get the same answer. From x − y = 2, x = y + 2; substituting into 2x + y = 7 gives 2(y + 2) + y = 7, so 3y = 3, y = 1, x = 3. Substitution is easier by hand for simple systems; determinants scale better.

Do the two equations have to be linear?

Yes for this method. If either equation contains x², y², xy, or a square root of x or y, the determinant formulas do not apply, and the system can have zero, one, two, or more solutions.

How do I check my answer?

Substitute x and y back into both original equations, as the page's check chips do. A correct solution makes both sides equal in both equations; a solution that satisfies only one equation is wrong.
