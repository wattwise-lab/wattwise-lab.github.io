Formula

ROI = (views × conversion% × order value × margin% − production cost) ÷ production cost × 100%. Orders = views × conversion%. Gross profit = orders × order value × margin%.

Worked example

Cost $1,800, views 50,000, conversion 0.8%, order value $199, margin 55%. Orders = 50,000 × 0.8% = 400. Revenue = 400 × $199 = $79,600. Gross profit = $79,600 × 55% = $43,780. Net = $43,780 − $1,800 = $41,980. ROI = $41,980 ÷ $1,800 × 100% = 2,332.22%. Break-even views at these rates are about 2,056 views (cost divided by profit per view of $0.8756: 0.8% x $199 x 55%).

Scope and limitations

Views and conversion are your assumptions, not a traffic forecast; a view count does not guarantee that conversion rate, and past content is only a rough guide. Revenue alone is not profit — the margin input is doing that work, so an overstated margin overstates ROI directly. Attribution is not modeled: orders credited to the piece may have been influenced by other channels, and production cost here is a single figure, excluding distribution, promotion, and revision costs unless you include them in it.

FAQ

How is ROI calculated?

Orders are views times the conversion rate; gross profit is orders times order value times margin; ROI is gross profit minus production cost, divided by production cost, times 100%.

What conversion rate should I use?

A measured one from comparable past content with the same offer and audience, not a platform average. If you have no measured rate, run the page at several rates and see how far ROI moves.

Why is margin in the formula?

Because revenue is not profit. Without margin, a high-revenue, low-margin product looks far better than it is. Enter the gross margin on the orders themselves, after product and fulfilment costs.

What counts as production cost?

Everything spent making the piece: creator time or fees, editing, props, locations, and licences. Add paid promotion separately if you want it counted — either include it in the cost or run a second scenario.

Can ROI exceed 1,000%?

Arithmetically yes, when a low-cost piece converts well, as in the worked example. That is a property of the ratio, not evidence the next piece will repeat it — small costs make the percentage swing wildly.

Does this forecast views?

No. Views are an input you supply. The page tells you what a given view count would be worth at your conversion and margin, not how many views you will get.
