Formula

ROI = (ending value + cash income − initial investment) ÷ initial investment × 100%. Gain = ending value + cash income − initial investment.

Worked example

Initial value $10,000, final value $12,500, cash income $500: gain = $12,500 + $500 − $10,000 = $3,000, and ROI = $3,000 ÷ $10,000 × 100% = 30.00%.

Scope and limitations

ROI does not account for how long capital was invested, so it cannot fairly compare investments held for different periods — use an annualized return for that. Taxes, fees, and inflation are not included unless they are already reflected in the values you enter.

FAQ

How does the ROI calculator work?

ROI = (ending value + cash income − initial investment) ÷ initial investment × 100%. It answers one question: for each dollar invested, how much came back above the original stake?

Why is my ROI negative?

A negative ROI means ending value plus cash income is less than the initial investment — you got back less than you put in. The size of the loss is the gain figure shown as a negative amount.

Can I compare two investments with ROI alone?

Only if they were held for the same length of time. A 30% return over one year beats 30% over five years. For different holding periods, compare annualized returns instead.

Should income be included?

Yes, if you received it — dividends, interest, or rent paid out during the holding period are part of the return. Do not double-count income that is already reflected in the final value.

Does ROI include taxes or fees?

No, unless the values you enter already reflect them. Brokerage fees, taxes on gains, and inflation all reduce the real return.

Is a high ROI always better?

Not necessarily. ROI says nothing about risk, volatility, or how long the money was tied up. A high ROI on a risky, illiquid investment is not directly comparable to a lower ROI on a safe one.
