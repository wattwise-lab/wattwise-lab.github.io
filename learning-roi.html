Formula

Payback months = (tuition + learning hours × hourly value) ÷ (expected monthly raise × probability%). Expected monthly benefit = expected monthly raise × probability%. Net after N months = expected monthly benefit × N − total investment.

Worked example

Tuition $12,000, 160 hours at $60, expected monthly gain $2,500 at 60% probability. Total investment = $12,000 + 160 × $60 = $21,600. Expected monthly benefit = $2,500 × 60% = $1,500. Payback = $21,600 ÷ $1,500 = 14.4 months. Net after 12 months = $1,500 × 12 − $21,600 = −$3,600; after 24 months = $1,500 × 24 − $21,600 = $14,400.

Scope and limitations

Expected income and probability are assumptions, not measured outcomes: a course provider's placement figure is not your probability. A zero expected monthly benefit has no finite financial payback, and the model assumes the gain starts immediately and never changes — real gains are delayed, partial, and may fade. Non-financial benefits (enjoyment, mobility, option value) are real but are not counted here, and neither are taxes on extra income, exam or material fees beyond the tuition input, or the risk of not completing the course.

FAQ

How is payback calculated?

Total investment (tuition plus study hours times hourly value) divided by the expected monthly benefit, which is the expected monthly raise times its probability.

Why include the value of study time?

Because hours spent learning have an opportunity cost. Ignoring them flatters expensive, time-heavy courses; valuing them at your hourly rate keeps the comparison honest.

What probability should I use?

Your honest estimate that you achieve the stated gain, given your background and the local market — not a provider's headline rate. Run the page at half your first guess to see how sensitive payback is.

What if the gain starts months later?

Add those months to the payback figure. The model assumes income starts at once; a six-month job search turns a 14.4-month payback into roughly 20.4 months from enrolment.

Does this cover non-financial benefits?

No. Satisfaction, career mobility, and the option to change fields are excluded. A course can fail this financial test and still be worth doing — but that should be a conscious choice, not an accident of the maths.

Why is there no payback when the gain is zero?

Because dividing by zero has no finite answer. If the expected gain is genuinely zero, the investment is never repaid by income, whatever its other merits.
