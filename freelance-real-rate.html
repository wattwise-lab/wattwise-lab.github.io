Formula

Hourly rate = (annual income target + expenses) ÷ [max(0.05, 1 − tax rate) × weekly hours × 48 × billable share]. Billable hours per year = weekly hours × 48 × billable share. Revenue needed = (income target + expenses) ÷ retained-income share.

Worked example

Income target $240,000, expenses $30,000, tax 20%, 40 hours/week, 60% billable: numerator = $270,000. Retained share = 1 − 0.20 = 0.80. Billable hours = 40 × 48 × 0.60 = 1,152 hours. Rate = $270,000 ÷ (0.80 × 1,152) = $270,000 ÷ 921.6 = $292.97/hour (292.96875 unrounded). Revenue needed = $270,000 ÷ 0.80 = $337,500; after 20% tax ($67,500) and $30,000 expenses, $240,000 remains. Scope and limitations

This simplified model uses a flat tax rate and a fixed 48 working weeks; it is not a jurisdiction-specific tax calculation and does not model progressive brackets, deductions, or self-employment taxes separately. The retained-income share is floored at 5% (max(0.05, 1 − tax rate)) so that extreme tax inputs do not divide by zero or a negative number — at tax rates above 95% the floor binds and the result is a floor-based estimate, not a tax-accurate one. Unpaid time (admin, marketing, holidays beyond the 48-week assumption) is captured only through the billable share.

FAQ

Why is the rate higher than income ÷ hours?

Because only the billable share of hours earns money, tax takes a share of revenue, and expenses must be covered on top of the income target. All three push the required rate above a simple division.

What is the 48-week assumption?

52 weeks less holidays and leave. It is fixed in this model; if you work fewer weeks, your billable hours fall and the required rate rises.

What does the 5% floor do?

It stops the retained share (1 − tax rate) from reaching zero or going negative at tax rates of 100% or more. Above a 95% tax rate the floor binds, so treat that result as illustrative only.

Is this my tax bill?

No. It uses one flat rate you enter. Real tax depends on brackets, deductions, and jurisdiction — this page is a planning estimate, not a tax calculation.

What if my billable share is very low?

The required rate rises sharply, because the same annual target must be earned in fewer billable hours. That is the correct signal: either raise the rate, raise the billable share, or lower the target.
